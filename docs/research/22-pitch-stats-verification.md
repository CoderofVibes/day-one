## Key facts — per claim

### a) Small manufacturers: 98% / 4.8M / 41%

**Claim:** “Small firms (<500 employees) are ~98% of US manufacturers; ~4.8M workers, ~41% of manufacturing employment”
**Cited source:** https://manufacturingleadgeneration.com/small-manufacturing-business-statistics/

**What the cited source actually says** (opened, lines verbatim in substance):
- Item 1: “There were **239,265 manufacturing firms** in the United States in 2022. All but 4,177 of them (**98.3%**) were small businesses with fewer than 500 employees. — US Census Bureau, Statistics of US Businesses”
- Item 2: “Small manufacturing firms represent **98% of all US manufacturers**, according to the SBA Office of Advocacy's 2025 manufacturing statistics report.”
- Item 6: “Small manufacturers (under 500 employees) employed **4.8 million workers**, representing about **40.9%** of total manufacturing employment. — SBA Office of Advocacy”
- Item 9 (important caveat on the same page): “The SBA defines a ‘small’ manufacturer as having fewer than 500 employees, **though size standards vary by NAICS code** and some manufacturing subsectors use revenue-based thresholds.” So “<500” is the general/profile definition, not a universal SBA size standard for every manufacturing subsector.

**Primary-source check:**
- SBA Office of Advocacy, *United States 2025* Small Business Profile (source: Statistics of U.S. Businesses, 2022, Census), industry table as reproduced in search results: **Manufacturing — 235,088 small employers, 98.3%, 4,984,670 small-business employees, 40.9%**. Definitions note on the profile: “Small businesses are generally defined here as firms with fewer than 500 employees.”
- SBA's own blog (Oct 2026): “**Small manufacturers account for 98 percent of all manufacturing firms in the United States**” — https://www.sba.gov/blog/2026/2026-10/national-veterans-small-business-week-2026-fueling-american-manufacturing-through-capital-growth-and-grit/
- A separate SBA announcement (Mar 2025) rounds differently: “About **99%** of American manufacturers are small businesses” — a press-release rounding, not the SUSB-based profile figure. Prefer 98% / 98.3% with the profile citation.

**Verdict: Partly verified.** Share (98% / 98.3%) ✔ and employment share (40.9% ≈ 41%) ✔ match primary data. Headcount: the cited page says 4.8M, but the primary profile table says **4,984,670 (≈5.0M)**. The two likely reflect different vintages/rounding in SBA Advocacy products; they cannot both be exact for the same year/definition. Do not pitch “4.8M” as an SBA/Census-exact figure.

**Safe rewording:** “Small manufacturers — firms with fewer than 500 employees — are **98.3% of US manufacturing firms and employ about 4.98 million people, 40.9% of manufacturing employment** (Census SUSB 2022, via SBA Office of Advocacy 2025 US profile).”

**Decision: Use with rewording.**

### b) Boomer-owned / silver tsunami: 40% / 3M+

**Claim:** “~40% of small businesses are Boomer-owned; 3M+ expected to change hands within a decade”
**Cited source:** https://impactalpha.com/cdfis-are-the-key-to-saving-3-million-small-businesses-from-silver-tsunami/
*Fetch note: the URL as given returned HTTP 404 on direct open on 2026-10-23. The article was verified via search-result full text for the canonical slug (http://impactalpha.com/cdfis-are-the-key-to-saving-3-million-small-businesses-from-the-silver-tsunami/) — treat the article-level check as search-verified, not full-page-opened.*

**What the source actually says** (per search-extracted article text):
- Author is Grow America Fund's President / Managing Director of Lending (an op-ed / advocacy piece by a CDFI lender, not a study).
- “Based on past data, **we expect that over three million small businesses will change hands within the next decade due to the retirement of their owners**.”
- “Together they represent **more than $10 trillion in business assets and tens of millions of jobs**.”
- **No “40% of small businesses are Boomer-owned” statement was found in the extracted article text.** The 40% figure appears elsewhere in the silver-tsunami ecosystem (e.g. a book-marketing site states “40% of small businesses Boomer-owned” without a primary citation in fetched text), but it is **not supported by the cited ImpactAlpha source as checked**.

**Primary-source check:**
- The standard underlying source is **Project Equity's analysis of US Census data**: Baby Boomers own **2.34 million businesses** (employer firms), employing **24.7 million people**, with **$5.14 trillion** in sales — reported by Nonprofit Quarterly and Project Equity-derived summaries. More recent secondary reporting of Project Equity / McKinsey's “Great Ownership Transfer” consistently uses **≈2.3 million** Boomer-owned employer businesses “representing one in six US jobs”, with other analyses running “as high as 3 million”.
- Note the denominator trap: 2.3M is a count of **employer** businesses. As a share of *all* US small businesses (36.2M per current SBA Advocacy homepage, mostly nonemployer firms) it is nowhere near 40%; as a share of employer firms it is much larger. Any “% Boomer-owned” claim is meaningless without stating its denominator — and no primary source fetched states 40% with a denominator.

**Verdict: Partly verified (3M+ as an expectation) / Unverified (40% as cited).** “Over 3 million will change hands in a decade” is genuinely in the cited article, but it is a lender-author's stated expectation (“we expect”, “based on past data” — data not shown), not a measured Census/SBA figure. The better-anchored count is ~2.3M Boomer-owned employer businesses (Project Equity / Census).

**Safe rewording:** “**About 2.3 million US employer businesses are owned by Baby Boomers approaching retirement — representing about one in six American jobs (Project Equity analysis of Census data). One CDFI lender, writing in ImpactAlpha, expects over 3 million small businesses to change hands within a decade.**” Drop the 40% unless a primary source with a stated denominator is found.

**Decision: Use with rewording (3M line, attributed as expectation + 2.3M primary anchor). Do not use the “~40% Boomer-owned” line.**

### c) Add-ons: 72.9% of US PE buyouts in 2025 (PitchBook)

**Claim:** “Add-ons were 72.9% of US PE buyouts by deal count in 2025 (PitchBook)”
**Cited source:** https://www.peony.ink/blog/add-on-acquisition-strategy

**What the cited source actually says** (opened):
- “They represented **72.9% of all US PE buyouts by deal count in full-year 2025**, essentially flat versus 73.1% in 2024 and in line with the five-year average of 72.8% (**PitchBook, 2025 Annual US PE Breakdown**). **That is count-based; by value the share is far smaller**, because add-ons are numerous and small.”
- The post is unusually careful about exactly the framing error to avoid: count ≠ value, and it warns against the fuzzy “72–75%” range circulating in secondary/AI sources.

**Primary-source check:**
- **PitchBook's own 2025 Annual US PE Breakdown PDF** (pitchbook.brightspotcdn.com) contains the chart “**Add-ons as a share of all PE buyouts**” with the series ending **73.1% → 72.9%** — matching the claim exactly.
- Independent PitchBook-derived analysis (Windsor Drake, citing PitchBook 2025 Annual US PE Breakdown, published 13 Jan 2026): “Add-ons were 72.9% of all US buyouts in 2025… **5,351 add-ons against 1,985 platform buyouts in 2025**” and, by value, “Add-on value 2025: $350.7 billion of $967.3 billion” (≈36.3% by value — their analysis of PitchBook components).
- Cross-check, different period, not a contradiction: CapitalPad reports **75.9% for Q2 2025 alone** (PitchBook via Cherry Bekaert). Quarterly ≠ full-year.

**Verdict: Verified.** Number, year, geography (US), metric (share of buyouts), and basis (deal count) all match PitchBook primary material.

**Safe rewording:** Not needed — pitch it exactly as: “**Add-ons were 72.9% of US PE buyouts by deal count in 2025 (PitchBook, 2025 Annual US PE Breakdown).**” Never drop “by deal count”.

**Decision: Use.**

### d) “Integration after close is where buy-and-build differentiates”

**Claim:** Qualitative — integration is the differentiator in buy-and-build.
**Cited source:** https://capitalpad.com/private-equity-roll-up-statistics/

**What the cited source actually says / supports** (opened in full):
CapitalPad's page does **not** contain that sentence as a finding, and presents no “integration differentiates” statistic. What it *does* compile, which supports the claim as an argument:
- Platform definition: “A good platform has the leadership, systems, and financial capacity to **absorb acquisitions** while continuing to serve its existing customers.”
- **Bain (2024), via CapitalPad:** buy-and-build deals 2010–2019 with a strategic rationale for **organic growth or margin improvement achieved 2.2x reported MOIC, vs 1.4x for those dependent on multiple arbitrage alone** (44 deals; subgroup sizes/fee basis not specified).
- **BCG/HHL (2016), via CapitalPad:** in a small, ~90% European, 1998–2012-exit sample, deals with **one or two add-ons averaged 35.5% IRR vs 19.9% for more than two** — with CapitalPad's own gloss: “**Matching acquisition pace to integration capacity**… The practical test is whether previous acquisitions are delivering the expected results.”
- A full section, “What a roll-up integration plan needs to cover”: “Buy-and-build execution requires a **repeatable process for making acquired businesses work better together**”, plus financing/integration-capacity discussion (“Debt service and integration spending both draw on company cash flow…”).
- Corroboration from the (c) source: Peony's “When multiple arbitrage fails” section names “**Integration debt**” as the first failure mode — “Integration is the work that earns the spread; skipping it forfeits the spread.” (Author experience, not data.)

CapitalPad is also transparent about limits: studies are observational, samples are old/European/small, and “none is presented as… a forecast”. Its commercial interest is disclosed on the page (it co-invests in lower-middle-market buy-and-build).

**Verdict: Partly verified — as a qualitative thesis, not as a sourced fact.** The direction (value comes from post-close operating/integration work, not arbitrage alone) is supported by the Bain comparison CapitalPad cites. The pitch sentence overstates if delivered as though CapitalPad (or any study) *measured* “integration” as the differentiator.

**Safe rewording:** “**The evidence points past the deal math: in Bain's analysis of 2010–2019 buy-and-build deals (via CapitalPad), programs built on organic growth and margin improvement returned 2.2x MOIC vs 1.4x for multiple arbitrage alone — the spread is earned in post-close integration, not at signing.**” Present the short pitch line as Day One's thesis, with this as its footnote — not the reverse.

**Decision: Use with rewording.**

### Combined framing line

**Claim:** “98% of American manufacturers are small businesses, employing nearly 5 million people”

**Check:** First clause = SBA blog verbatim (“Small manufacturers account for 98 percent of all manufacturing firms”) and SBA Advocacy profile (98.3%). Second clause = SBA Advocacy / Census SUSB 2022 profile figure **4,984,670** small-manufacturing employees — “nearly 5 million” is an accurate rounding of the *primary* figure, and is actually better supported than the “4.8M” variant in claim (a).

**Verdict: Verified** (with the general <500-employee profile definition; note SBA size standards vary by NAICS subsector if challenged).

**Decision: Use** — recommended citation footnote: “SBA Office of Advocacy, 2025 US Small Business Profile (Census SUSB 2022): 98.3% of manufacturing firms are small (<500 employees), employing 4.98M people (40.9% of manufacturing employment).”
